Factory vs trader

A factory owns production lines, tooling and QC. A trader sources from factories and resells, adding a margin and a layer of risk. Both can deliver a first order. The difference shows up at the worst possible moments: when a colour comes out wrong and someone has to stand at the line and re-run it, or when a shipment slips and there is nobody to escalate to who controls the schedule. Direct pricing, faster sampling and real accountability are the factory's side of the ledger.

Proof a real factory provides

  • Business license with a manufacturing scope, not just trading
  • Facility photos and video of actual production, not product renders
  • Current third-party audit reports: ISO 9001, BSCI, SGS material tests
  • A live video-call factory tour, on request, without a script

Red flags to avoid

  • No facility video, or "can't show the floor for confidentiality"
  • Vague answers about production process and capacity
  • Prices that swing wildly with quantity without an explained cost base
  • No verifiable export track record to your market

Any one of these might have an innocent explanation. Two of them together usually mean you are talking to a salesperson, not a maker.

Questions to ask before paying a deposit

  1. Do you own the production lines, or source and resell? A factory names its lines; a trader names a supplier.
  2. Can I see a live production video, not a brochure?
  3. Which certifications are current — ISO 9001, BSCI, REACH test reports for my material?
  4. Will you share batch-traceability records after the order ships?
  5. What exactly happens if the bulk run drifts from the approved sample?

The MOQ is a colour-change rule, not a policy

Most buyers treat the quoted minimum as fixed. It is not. For TPE mats, 500 pcs is roughly the shortest run that justifies one custom colour, because the line has to be purged between colours and that cost needs volume to disappear into. Ask for three scenarios instead of one number: stock colour with a laser-engraved logo (usually 100–200 pcs), custom Pantone with your own size (500+), and full-colour heat-transfer print (300+ before it turns economical). If the number does not move when you offer to take a stock colour, you are not talking to the factory.

Specify density and gram weight, not just thickness

"6 mm TPE" is not a specification. Two mats can both measure 6 mm and differ by 30 percent in material content — invisible in photographs, obvious in returns. Ask for density plus the finished weight per mat in grams, and get tolerances in writing: on our line, ±1 mm on length and width and ±0.3 mm on thickness. Then weigh the production sample yourself. A mat that comes in 200 g lighter than the approved sample is a downgrade, whatever the invoice says.

Five tests to run on the pre-production sample

  • Dry grip, then wet grip with a spray bottle. This is where cheap TPE fails.
  • Odour: seal it in a bag for 48 hours, then open it. That is the smell your customer gets.
  • Curl memory: unroll it and wait 24 hours. Ends that refuse to lie flat are the top complaint from studio buyers.
  • Weigh it against the quoted gram weight.
  • Fold it hard once and inspect the crease.

Then keep one signed sample at your office and one at the factory. Realistically, that pair is your only leverage if the bulk run drifts.

Payment terms that protect both sides

A balanced order uses a deposit to lock material and a balance on sight of shipping documents or before container release. Be wary of any supplier demanding 100 percent upfront, and equally of one refusing any deposit at all. A real manufacturer is comfortable with 30% deposit / 70% against bill of lading, because it has the facility and inventory to start work without holding your full payment.

Certificates: ask by market, and ask for your product

  • EU: REACH SVHC declaration; add EN 71-3 if the mat is marketed to children.
  • US: California Prop 65 assessment.
  • Any recycled-content claim: GRS, with a transaction certificate.

Testing is done per material batch, not per factory, and not forever. A report with no product spec, no date, or a different company name on it is not evidence. Our compliance documentation is described in detail in the compliance guide.

Inspect before you pay the balance

AQL 2.5 / 4.0 is the usual standard, and a third-party inspector costs roughly US$200–300 a day, which is cheap insurance on your first two orders. At the very least, get three pieces pulled from the actual production batch, photographs of the packed cartons, and a print colour check against your Pantone reference. Do this before the balance moves, not after.

The timeline nobody quotes you

Sampling takes 7–12 days depending on how complete your spec is. Bulk production runs 15–25 days for a first order of 100–1,000 pcs, longer for full-container programs. Ocean freight adds 25–40 days to the US West Coast or Europe. That is 50–80 days from first email to your warehouse. A supplier promising 20 days door-to-door is shipping somebody else's stock, which means you are not getting a custom product at all.

Compare landed cost, not unit price

Get EXW, FOB and DDP quoted side by side. A cheap EXW price routinely ends up more expensive on arrival once you add inland haulage, export clearance and a forwarder who has never shipped mats before. Ask for pieces per 40HQ and check the number against your own carton dimensions; under-quoted container capacity is the quietest way a low unit price hides a higher landed cost. And below roughly 500 mats, air freight eats the entire margin.

FAQ

How do I tell a factory from a trading company?

Ask for the business licence and check that its registered scope includes manufacturing, not just trading. Then ask to see the production equipment under the company's own name, and request a live video walk-through of the foaming and finishing lines. A trader can usually show a showroom but hesitates when asked to point a camera at the foaming machine.

Should I pay for a third-party audit?

For a first bulk order, yes. An audit by SGS, TUV or Intertek costs far less than a failed shipment and verifies what a supplier presentation cannot. For repeat orders with a proven supplier, a documented self-audit plus batch test reports is often sufficient.

What documents should a real factory provide?

Business licence with manufacturing scope, ISO 9001 certificate, BSCI or equivalent social-compliance audit if you sell into the EU, current third-party material test reports for your market, and batch traceability records. A genuine factory produces these within a day or two because it already has them.

Is a video factory tour enough?

A live, unscripted video call is a strong signal and filters out most traders, but it does not replace documentation or an audit. Use video to shortlist, then rely on certificates, test reports and a trial order before committing to volume.

Is 500 pcs really the minimum for a custom yoga mat?

Rarely. 500 pcs is the shortest run that justifies one custom colour, not a factory policy. If you accept a stock colour and only customise the logo by laser engraving, 100–200 pcs is usually achievable; custom Pantone plus custom size is what pushes it to 500+. Ask for the MOQ in three scenarios before you accept the first number.

How long does it take from first enquiry to delivery?

Plan for 50–80 days. Sampling takes 7–12 days, bulk production 15–25 days for a first order of 100–1,000 pcs, and ocean freight another 25–40 days to the US West Coast or Europe. Anyone promising 20 days door-to-door is shipping stock that already exists, not making your product.

EXW, FOB or DDP — which should a first-time buyer choose?

Get all three quoted and compare landed cost, not unit price. EXW looks cheapest and frequently ends up the most expensive once inland haulage, export clearance and your forwarder's fees are added. FOB is the sensible default for a first order; DDP is worth its premium only if you do not want to touch customs at all.
